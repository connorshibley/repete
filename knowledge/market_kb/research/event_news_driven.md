# Event-Driven and News-Driven Equity Trading: Evidence and Data-Source Guide

*Research compiled August 2026. All claims below are cited to primary academic sources or product/documentation pages fetched directly.*

## Part A — Strategy Evidence

### A.1 Post-Earnings Announcement Drift (PEAD)

**The anomaly.** PEAD is one of the oldest and most replicated anomalies in market-efficiency research. It was first documented by Ray Ball and Philip Brown in their 1968 study "An Empirical Evaluation of Accounting Income Numbers" in the *Journal of Accounting Research* (vol. 6, pp. 159-178) — a paper widely regarded as the founding work of empirical capital-markets accounting research ([Ball and Brown (1968) After Fifty Years, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/SSRN_ID3304777_code17468.pdf?abstractid=3304777&mirid=1&type=2)). Ball and Brown found that more than 80% of the information content of an earnings announcement is already reflected in price by the time of the announcement, yet — counter to strict market efficiency — a residual drift in the same direction as the earnings surprise persists for roughly two months afterward, as documented in the retrospective discussion of the study at [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/podcast/knowledge-at-wharton-podcast/jacobs-levy-award-2019/).

Victor Bernard and Jacob Thomas formalized and extended this in "Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift: Delayed Price Response or Risk Premium?" (*Journal of Accounting Research*, 1989, vol. 27, pp. 1-36), studying NYSE/AMEX firms from 1974-1986 sorted into deciles by standardized unexpected earnings (SUE) ([study record, OA Monitor Ireland](https://oamonitor.ireland.openaire.eu/rfo/sfi_rfo/search/publication?pid=10.2307/2491062)). A long-top-decile/short-bottom-decile SUE hedge portfolio produced approximately 4.2 percentage points of return difference over the 60 trading days following earnings, translating to roughly 25% annualized returns before transaction costs — a magnitude not explained by beta, firm size, or the January effect ([Alpha Suite summary of the Foster/Olsen/Shevlin and Bernard/Thomas findings](https://alpha-suite.org/blog/post-earnings-announcement-drift)). A widely cited follow-up finding from Bernard and Thomas (1990) is that 25-30% of the total drift is concentrated in the three-day windows around the firm's *next* quarterly earnings announcement — i.e., a small number of trading days (about 5% of the calendar) capture a disproportionate share of the effect, as summarized in the [Wikipedia entry on post-earnings-announcement drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift).

**Constructing a rule.** The canonical implementation, drawn directly from the Bernard-Thomas design: rank all stocks that just reported earnings by standardized unexpected earnings (SUE) — the earnings surprise divided by the standard deviation of historical surprises, or simply consensus-beating percentage magnitude; go long the top decile (largest positive surprises) and short (or simply avoid/underweight) the bottom decile; hold the position for approximately 60 trading days (roughly one quarter, i.e., until just before/through the next earnings announcement), since that is the horizon over which the original hedge-portfolio returns were measured ([Alpha Suite](https://alpha-suite.org/blog/post-earnings-announcement-drift); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%E2%80%93earnings-announcement_drift)).

**Does it still work today? Mixed, contested evidence.** The magnitude has clearly shrunk from its 1970s-80s levels — modern estimates put the quarterly drift closer to 1.5-3%, concentrated in smaller, less-analyst-covered stocks rather than large caps ([Alpha Suite](https://alpha-suite.org/blog/post-earnings-announcement-drift)). More strikingly, a body of 2020s research argues the effect has effectively disappeared outside of microcaps. Kevin Martineau's paper "Rest in Peace, Post-Earnings Announcement Drift" finds PEAD vanished from non-microcap U.S. stocks after 2001 and was fully gone by 2006 ([SocArXiv/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/osf/socarx/z7k3p.html)). This directly conflicts with two 2025 papers (Dickerson, Julliard & Mueller; and Hirshleifer, Peng & Wang) that re-assert PEAD's continued existence — but a replication effort by Avanidhar Subrahmanyam found the effect's t-statistic falls from 2.18 to an insignificant 1.43 once microcap stocks are excluded from the sample, as summarized in [UCLA Anderson Review's "Is Post-Earnings Announcement Drift a Thing? Again?"](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/is-post-earnings-announcement-drift-a-thing-again/). A 2026 paper in the *Journal of Investment Management* corroborates the "PEAD is mostly a microcap phenomenon" conclusion with the same t-stat of 1.43 for non-microcap samples post-2000 ([Science Publishing Group, "Keeping It Simple"](https://sciencepublishinggroup.com/article/10.11648/j.jim.20261501.11)). On the other side, controlled laboratory/experimental evidence published in the *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis* finds PEAD-like underreaction is a robust, reproducible behavioral phenomenon even outside noisy real-market data ([Cambridge University Press, "Earnings Autocorrelation and the Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/61CD6A2065A4686418A3C47DEF3AC24B/S0022109023000881a.pdf/earnings_autocorrelation_and_the_postearningsannouncement_drift_experimental_evidence.pdf)).

**Practical takeaway:** PEAD as a large-cap, liquid strategy is likely arbitraged away; any residual edge is concentrated in small/micro-cap, low-analyst-coverage names where transaction costs and capacity constraints limit institutional exploitation — precisely the segment retail-scale agents can access but large funds cannot efficiently trade at scale.

### A.2 Earnings-Reaction and Guidance-Revision Trading (Estimate Revisions / "Earnings Momentum")

Analyst estimate-revision strategies are a close cousin of PEAD but trade on the trajectory of Wall Street's own forecasts rather than the reported number itself. The data infrastructure for this research is the Institutional Brokers' Estimate System (I/B/E/S, originally a Lynch, Jones & Ryan product later owned by Thomson Reuters/Refinitiv), a historical consensus-estimate database covering over 70,000 companies across 90+ countries used extensively in the academic literature ([Tilburg University I/B/E/S overview](https://www.tilburguniversity.edu/sites/default/files/download/IBESonWRDS_2.pdf)).

The core empirical finding: firms whose analyst estimates are being revised *upward* systematically outperform firms being revised *downward*, and this signal is at least as powerful as — and partly independent of — the earnings-surprise (PEAD) signal. One study found that buying the highest-aggregated-forecast-revision decile and shorting the lowest produces a 90-day abnormal return of 3.4%, compared to 1.8-2.2% for pure earnings-surprise-based strategies over the same horizon ([UC eScholarship working paper](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2r7980f3/qt2r7980f3_noSplash_c002fc5c3b5de2401d705fadd922a1f3.pdf)). Combining analyst recommendation *changes* with earnings estimate revisions compounds the effect further: Kecskés, Michaely, and Womack, in a *Management Science* paper, document risk-adjusted returns of roughly 3% per month from portfolios that jointly weight recommendation changes and estimate revisions ([IDEAS/RePEc record of the Management Science paper](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v63y2017i6p1855-1871.html)).

**The commercial track record: Zacks Rank.** Zacks Investment Research operationalizes estimate-revision momentum into a 1-5 stock ranking system, re-ranked weekly on roughly 4,400 tickers, based on the trajectory of "discounted" (i.e., forward-adjusted) future earnings estimates ([ZacksPro Rank methodology manual](https://www.zackspro.com/Manuals/Estimates_ZacksRank.pdf)). Per Zacks' own performance disclosure — independently examined by the accounting firm Cherry Bekaert LLP for the 2022-2024 period — Zacks Rank #1 ("Strong Buy") stocks have returned an average annualized +23.94% from 1988 through July 2026, versus +11.55% annualized for the S&P 500 over the same period ([Zacks Performance Disclosure](https://www.zacks.com/performance_disclosure/)). A corroborating source, Zacks' own trading guide, states Rank #1 stocks beat the market in 29 of the last 37 years with an average annual return of +23.5% versus +11.0% for the S&P 500 (1988-May 2025) ([Zacks Trade Rank Guide PDF](https://www.zackstrade.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/zacks-rank-guide-2022-09.pdf)). These are vendor-published, not independently peer-reviewed, figures and should be treated with the appropriate skepticism reserved for a commercial track record — but the underlying academic literature on estimate revisions (above) gives the mechanism independent support.

### A.3 Macro / Fed-Announcement Reaction Strategies

**Pre-FOMC drift.** David Lucca and Emanuel Moench's Federal Reserve Bank of New York staff report "The Pre-FOMC Announcement Drift" (Staff Report No. 512) is the seminal paper here: since 1994, the S&P 500 has risen on average about 49 basis points in the 24 hours *before* scheduled FOMC announcements, and this pre-announcement drift accounts for more than 80% of the entire average equity risk premium earned over the 17-year sample period studied — with no equivalent effect found in Treasury or money-market instruments ([NY Fed Staff Report 512 PDF](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr512.pdf); summary indexed at [Fed in Print](https://fedinprint.org/item/fednsr/12655)).

**Has it decayed?** Yes, substantially. A study titled "The Disappearing Pre-FOMC Announcement Drift" finds the effect essentially vanished after 2015, a change the authors attribute to reduced monetary-policy uncertainty (forward guidance, more predictable Fed communication) ([PMC/NIH-hosted version](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7525326/); also published in [ScienceDirect / Journal of Financial Markets](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1544612320315956)).

**The broader FOMC-day effect persists.** A 2026 Federal Reserve Board FEDS working paper finds the average excess stock return on macro/FOMC announcement days is 11.4 basis points, versus just 1.1 basis points on non-announcement days — with more than 60% of the entire realized average equity premium accruing on FOMC announcement days specifically ([Federal Reserve Board FEDS paper 2026-023](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2026023pap.pdf)). Quantpedia's summary of the "FOMC meeting effect" strategy corroborates that average S&P 500 daily returns during FOMC-meeting windows since 1980 are more than five times greater than average non-meeting days, with over 16% of a typical year's total equity return realized on this small handful of days ([Quantpedia, FOMC Meeting Effect in Stocks](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/federal-open-market-committee-meeting-effect-in-stocks)).

**CPI / jobs reports.** The New York Fed's "How Economic News Moves Markets" (Current Issues series) finds that among the many scheduled macro releases, only three generate economically significant and persistent price responses: the nonfarm payrolls (jobs) report, the GDP advance release, and the ISM Manufacturing Report; bond yields react most strongly to these releases, with equities reacting somewhat less ([NY Fed Current Issues 14-6 PDF](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/current_issues/ci14-6.pdf)).

**Practical takeaway:** A systematic "trade the drift" approach naively long into FOMC days would have captured a real historical premium through roughly 2015, but the pre-announcement drift specifically has decayed; the FOMC-day *volatility and return concentration* effect (as opposed to a directional pre-drift) appears more durable per the 2026 Fed paper.

### A.4 Merger Arbitrage / M&A Event Strategies

Merger arbitrage (risk arbitrage) — buying a target's stock after a merger is announced and capturing the spread between deal price and market price, sometimes pairing with a short in the acquirer for stock deals — is one of the most extensively documented "event-driven" strategies precisely because its return stream behaves differently from broad equities.

Mitchell and Pulvino's "Characteristics of Risk and Return in Risk Arbitrage" (*Journal of Finance*, 2001, vol. 56, no. 6, pp. 2135-2175) analyzed 4,750 mergers between 1963 and 1998 ([EconPapers/RePEc abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/blajfinan/v_3a56_3ay_3a2001_3ai_3a6_3ap_3a2135-2175.htm); full text via [SSRN mirror](https://assets.super.so/e46b77e7-ee08-445e-b43f-4ffd88ae0a0e/files/69fbb019-259d-48e9-a2de-f17d1161cf12.pdf)). Their key finding: merger-arb returns are positively correlated with the broad market only during severely depreciating markets, and are essentially uncorrelated with the market during flat or appreciating periods — a payoff profile resembling a short put option on the market. They estimate excess (risk-adjusted) returns of roughly 4% per year after transaction costs. A Penn State honors thesis citing the same dataset reports gross annual abnormal returns of 9.25% over 1963-1998 for risk arbitrage before costs ([Penn State honors thesis](https://honors.libraries.psu.edu/files/final_submissions/9704)).

More recent live-fund evidence: the HFRI Event Driven: Merger Arbitrage Index posted an annualized return of 7.34% with volatility of just 4.3% and correlation to the S&P 500 of only 54% from January 1990 through November 2022, with only two negative calendar years across more than three decades ([Neuberger Berman merger-arbitrage index summary](https://www.nb.com/handlers/documents.ashx?id=b598dbd5-013f-4578-b8e4-76a0481776f4)). A New York Fed staff report on merger options frames the mechanism: arbitrageurs earn the spread as compensation for providing liquidity and bearing deal-completion risk, with a nonlinear payoff — small steady gains if the deal closes, large losses if it breaks ([NY Fed Staff Report 761](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr761.pdf?la=en)). Alpha Architect's review of merger arbitrage as a portfolio diversifier cites Verdad's proprietary database of 835 deals from 2000-2020 in support of the strategy's low-correlation, positive-carry characteristics ([Alpha Architect, "Merger Arbitrage as a Diversification Strategy"](https://alphaarchitect.com/merger-spread/)).

**Practical takeaway:** merger arb is not a "news trading" strategy in the sub-second sense — it is a slower, fundamentals-driven event strategy where the "news" is the initial deal announcement (requiring fast detection and entry before the spread compresses) followed by a multi-week/multi-month hold managing deal-break risk, regulatory timelines, and spread dynamics.

### A.5 Known Risks: Efficient Markets, Latency, Headline Risk, Over/Underreaction

**Speed and the latency disadvantage.** The empirical evidence that speed matters enormously is unambiguous. A Federal Reserve International Finance Discussion Paper using RavenPack news-analytics data finds that the stock-price reaction concentrated in the first five seconds after a news release is 1.3 percentage points higher (about 10% relative to the mean) for high-relevance, high-novelty articles — and RavenPack itself delivers sentiment/relevance metrics roughly 300 milliseconds after the underlying Dow Jones Newswire release, a speed no human trader can match ([Federal Reserve IFDP working paper](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/ifdp/files/ifdp1233.pdf); accessible summary at [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/high-frequency-trading-profiting-news/) and [World Economic Forum](https://www.weforum.org/stories/economic-growth/how-does-news-analytics-affect-the-stock-market/)). A study in the *Journal of Banking & Finance* on earnings announcements finds algorithmic traders profit in the first 90 seconds post-announcement while non-algorithmic (human/retail) traders systematically lose money in that same window ([ScienceDirect study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927538X16300531)).

The economics of latency are quantified directly in "Speed, Algorithmic Trading, and Market Quality around Macroeconomic News": a 300-millisecond execution delay reduces returns by 3.08% relative to instantaneous execution around a macro news release, and a full one-second delay costs 7.33% — while HFT firms using co-located infrastructure achieve round-trip latencies below 100 microseconds ([Econstor working paper](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/87562/1/12-121.pdf)). Related work summarized in an Oxford Journal piece finds HFT firms are responsible for roughly 70% of the price reaction occurring in the first ten seconds after a news release (Martinez & Rosu), and that stocks with high HFT participation incorporate new information about 1.5x faster than low-HFT stocks (Zhang, 2010) ([Oxford Journal summary](https://www.oxjournal.org/assessing-the-impact-of-high-frequency-trading-on-market-efficiency-and-stability/)).

**Implication for a retail-scale agent:** competing on raw sub-second latency against co-located HFT infrastructure is not viable. The realistic edge windows are (a) the slower PEAD/estimate-revision drift horizons (days to months) where speed is irrelevant, (b) less-followed small/micro-cap names where institutional and HFT competition is thin, and (c) structural/fundamental event categories like merger arbitrage where the relevant "reaction time" is hours to days, not milliseconds.

**Headline risk and false signals.** Because so much modern trading is driven by automated headline/keyword parsing, algorithmic systems are vulnerable to being triggered by misleading, retracted, or algorithmically-generated fake headlines — a widely discussed but harder-to-quantify risk noted alongside the news-analytics literature above ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/high-frequency-trading-profiting-news/)); any production trading agent needs source-credibility filtering and duplicate/retraction detection layered on top of any headline-triggered strategy.

**Overreaction vs. underreaction — the coexistence puzzle.** Two seemingly contradictory anomalies coexist in the literature: De Bondt and Thaler's (1985) overreaction/long-term-reversal hypothesis (past big losers subsequently outperform past big winners over 3-5 year horizons) versus Jegadeesh and Titman's (1993) momentum/underreaction finding (past 3-12 month winners keep winning). Barberis, Shleifer, and Vishny's (1998) behavioral model reconciles the two by proposing investors underreact to isolated pieces of news (consistent with short-horizon momentum and PEAD) but overreact to a consistent pattern of news in one direction (consistent with long-horizon reversal) — summarized in the [behavioral-finance overreaction/underreaction reference site](http://overreaction-and-underreaction.behaviouralfinance.net/) and reviewed in a [French doctoral thesis on the topic](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-03037432/document). For an event-driven agent, the practical implication is horizon-dependence: news-driven signals should generally be traded with a "ride the drift, don't fight it" posture over days-to-months, while being alert that very large, emotionally salient moves (huge single-day rallies/crashes) are the setups most likely to mean-revert over multi-year horizons.

### B.c — Regulatory / Company Filings Sources

- **SEC EDGAR Submissions API** (official, free, no key required): `https://data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK##########.json` returns an entity's full filing history (current/former names, exchanges, tickers) and is updated **in real time throughout the day** as filings are disseminated, with typical processing delay under one second (longer during peak filing periods) ([SEC EDGAR APIs official documentation](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces)).
- **SEC EDGAR XBRL Company Concept / Company Facts APIs** (official, free): `https://data.sec.gov/api/xbrl/companyconcept/CIK##########/us-gaap/{tag}.json` and the companyfacts equivalent return structured historical financial data points extracted from XBRL-tagged filings ([SEC EDGAR APIs official documentation](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces)).
- **SEC EDGAR Full-Text Search API (EFTS)** (official, free, undocumented-but-public JSON endpoint): `https://efts.sec.gov/LATEST/search-index?q=<phrase>` searches the full body text of every EDGAR filing (10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, S-1, DEF 14A, SC 13D/G, Form 4, etc.) filed since 2001; supports `forms`, `startdt`/`enddt`, `ciks`, and `entityName` filters; requires a declared `User-Agent` header (with contact email) or requests are rejected with a 403, and is subject to a fair-access cap of roughly 10 requests/second ([SEC's own Full-Text Search FAQ](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/efts-faq.html); endpoint mechanics documented in third-party technical write-ups: [The Mine Works EFTS guide](https://themineworks.com/blog/sec-edgar-full-text-search-api/), [EDGARScout API documentation](https://edgarscout.com/edgar-api/)). The consumer-facing search page at [sec.gov/edgar/search](https://www.sec.gov/edgar//search/) calls this same backend.
- **Bulk nightly archive:** a full nightly-recompiled ZIP of all EDGAR submission histories is published at `https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/daily-index/bulkdata/submissions.zip`, refreshed around 3:00 a.m. ET daily — useful for building a local historical filings database without hammering the live API ([SEC EDGAR APIs official documentation](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces)).
- **Commercial alternative (paid, lower-latency):** sec-api.io offers a hosted full-text search API (`POST https://api.sec-api.io/full-text-search`) that claims to index new EDGAR filings as searchable within under 60 seconds of publication, useful if the free EFTS endpoint's rate limits or reliability become a bottleneck ([sec-api.io Full-Text Search API documentation](https://sec-api.io/docs/full-text-search-api)).

### B.d — Macro Calendars

- **FOMC Meeting Calendar** (official, free): published at [federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm); lists all scheduled meeting dates and flags which meetings include a Summary of Economic Projections and press conference. The Fed also publishes an official FOMC blackout-period calendar (the window before each meeting when Fed officials refrain from public commentary), useful for a trading agent that wants to model information-flow suppression ahead of announcements ([FOMC Blackout Period Calendar PDF](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/fomc-blackout-period-calendar.pdf)).
- **BLS Schedule of Releases** (official, free): [bls.gov/schedule](https://www.bls.gov/schedule/) publishes exact release dates/times for CPI, the Employment Situation (monthly jobs report, released 8:30 a.m. ET the third Friday after the reference week), PPI, JOLTS, and the Employment Cost Index; a machine-readable ICS calendar feed is available at `bls.gov/schedule/news_release/bls.ics` for direct calendar integration.
- **BEA Release Schedule** (official, free): [bea.gov/news/schedule](https://www.bea.gov/news/schedule) covers GDP estimates (advance/second/third releases), Personal Income and Outlays, and international trade data release dates — complements BLS for a complete macro-calendar feed.

### B.e — Social / Alt-Data Sentiment Sources

- **StockTwits API:** StockTwits exposes public, unauthenticated read endpoints for per-symbol message streams, e.g. `https://api.stocktwits.com/api/2/streams/symbol/{SYMBOL}.json`, and a trending-symbols endpoint at `/trending/symbols.json`, both usable without an API key for light read access ([community-documented usage on GitHub](https://github.com/hannesbarbez/BarbezDotEu.StockTwits); [StockTwits Trending API OpenAPI spec](https://apis.io/apis/stocktwits/stocktwits-trending-api/)). Higher-volume/authenticated access (posting, full stream access, higher rate limits) requires applying for "partner level" developer access directly with StockTwits. A separate premium product, **Firestream**, offers a real-time HTTP-Basic-authenticated streaming feed of the full StockTwits message and sentiment firehose (including a pre-computed sentiment-score endpoint per symbol), aimed at institutional/data-vendor customers rather than casual developers ([Firestream Documentation portal](https://firestream-portal.stocktwits.com/)). StockTwits does not publish numeric pricing for either tier on its public developer pages; a subscriber-facing pricing page mentions only that annual plans save "up to 16%" versus monthly, with no listed dollar figures ([StockTwits developer/subscriptions page](https://stocktwits.com/developers)) — treat this as free-to-low-cost for basic read access and enterprise-negotiated for Firestream.
- **X (Twitter) API:** As of February 2026, X eliminated its free tier for new developers and moved to a pay-per-use default: $0.005 per post read, $0.015 per post created ($0.20 if the post contains a link), capped at 2 million reads/month before requiring an Enterprise contract (~$42,000+/month). Legacy fixed-price subscriptions — Basic at $200/month and Pro at $5,000/month — remain available only to developers who were already subscribed before the change ([Postproxy X API pricing breakdown](https://postproxy.dev/blog/x-api-pricing-2026/); corroborating detail on the reads cap and "owned reads" pricing at [Data365 X API pricing guide](https://data365.co/guides/twitter-api-limitations-and-pricing); official tier descriptions at [X Developer Platform](https://developer.x.com/en)). For a solo developer wanting finance-account/keyword monitoring at meaningful volume, this makes X materially more expensive than the other sources in this guide; third-party resellers (e.g., TwitterAPI.io) offer per-1,000-tweet pricing around $0.15/1K as a lower-cost (though unofficial, ToS-adjacent) alternative ([TwitterAPI.io pricing](https://twitterapi.io/pricing)).
- **Reddit WallStreetBets (WSB) sentiment — academic evidence is mixed-to-negative on tradability.** A University of Gothenburg master's thesis finds WSB sentiment has a statistically significant correlation with meme-stock returns specifically, using OLS and panel regressions on aggregated daily Reddit data ([GUPEA thesis repository](https://gupea.ub.gu.se/handle/2077/72514?show=full)). A *Digital Finance* journal study of the three largest finance subreddits (wallstreetbets, investing, stocks) finds that a strategy of buying Reddit-recommended stocks weighted by daily post volume does produce higher average returns than the market — but with **unfavorable Sharpe ratios**, i.e., the extra return does not compensate for the extra risk taken ([Digital Finance / RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/digfin/v5y2023i2d10.1007_s42521-023-00084-y.html)). A University of Oxford working paper goes further, concluding WSB sentiment and "due diligence" posts provide little to no predictive signal for future returns outside of a narrow set of meme stocks like GME and AMC, and explicitly warns that retail investors following WSB sentiment/advice risk substantial losses given the forum's bias toward high-risk positions ([Oxford working paper, "Is there good investment advice on r/wallstreetbots?"](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:6f3c53d3-f0d6-4232-b3cf-bf1696b200f9/files/dsf268587w)). A more recent (2025) arXiv study using a BERTweet-based "Sentiment Volume Change" metric on over 2 million WSB comments reports improved next-day-return correlation versus raw sentiment alone, suggesting volume-weighted sentiment shifts may carry more signal than sentiment polarity by itself ([arXiv 2508.02089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02089)).

**Overall takeaway for Part B.e:** social/alt-data sentiment is the least reliable data category in this guide — the most rigorous academic study (Oxford) found essentially no general predictive value outside of a handful of meme stocks, and even the studies finding *some* signal report poor risk-adjusted returns. For a production trading agent, social sentiment is best used as a secondary confirmation/risk-flag signal (e.g., detecting unusual retail-attention spikes that might precede a short squeeze or halt) rather than as a primary alpha source.
